I failed No iPad November

 

I need to confess something. I failed No iPad November.

We reduced the screens but absolutely did not eliminate them. And my kids have a new favourite insult.

Not in a cute, “We slipped up once because it was raining” way.

We reduced the iPads, yes. We had more limits and we said no more often. But we did not eliminate them, and we are nowhere near the screen-free family I imagined we would become.

This is particularly humbling because last year, I practically became a No iPad November evangelist.

The kids barely missed them.They were kinder, funnier and more interested in us.

I remember thinking: It’s like we got our kids back.

So naturally, I assumed we could do it again.

Apparently, I was drunk on last year’s success.

This year has been harder, Life is fuller, the baby is older and louder. The older kids have stronger opinions, I am more tired. Sometimes an iPad is the only reason I can make dinner, answer an email, help another child or sit silently for seven consecutive minutes.

And my kids have now started using “iPad kid” as an insult.

“You’re such an iPad kid.”

“At least I’m not an iPad kid.”

This is sometimes said by a child who was holding an iPad less than four minutes earlier.

They understand the cultural shame of being an iPad kid while simultaneously campaigning to become one full-time.

Honestly, that feels like a fairly accurate summary of our entire relationship with technology.

We know too much screen time isn’t ideal and we see the behaviour shift when it is time to turn it off. We notice how quickly boredom has become an emergency and I watch them reach for a device before their imaginations have even had a chance to wake up.

And then we hand it back anyway because we are trying to survive dinner.

The statistics make me feel slightly less alone and significantly more concerned.

In 2024, 54% of Canadian children and youth between 3 and 17 exceeded recreational screen-time guidelines. The average was about 2.8 hours a day. Among children ages 5 to 11, the percentage meeting the recommended limit dropped from 73% before the pandemic to 62% between 2022 and 2024. ​www150.statcan.gc.ca​

And parents know this is hard. A recent Canadian study found that more than eight in ten parents believe limiting screen time is important, nearly two-thirds are concerned about their child’s usage, and a majority say controlling it is a challenge. ​onlinelibrary.wiley.com​

So this is not a problem caused by a small group of lazy parents who can’t say no.

This is a defining challenge of modern parenthood.

Our children are growing up with entertainment designed to never end. There is always another level, another video and another thing waiting to reward their brains.

And we are trying to compete with it using puzzles and a cheerful suggestion to “go play outside.”

Meanwhile, we are also on our own phones. We work on them, organize our families on them, communicate through them and occasionally hide in the bathroom with them.

The Canadian Paediatric Society doesn’t tell families that all screen use is bad. It recommends looking at whether screens are interfering with sleep, school, face-to-face connection, physical activity and offline play. It also reminds us that our children are watching our habits too, which feels both reasonable and personally offensive. ​Caring for kids​

I don’t want to be judgmental about this because I am standing directly inside the problem.

Screens can be educational. They can help a child regulate. They can give an exhausted parent a needed break. For some families and some children, they are an important tool.

But I also don’t want to pretend everything is fine because the truth feels uncomfortable.

I worry that the “iPad kid” is not really a type of child.

It is a generation of children, including mine, being raised inside a giant experiment for which none of us has the final results.

Maybe this is one of the biggest epidemics of our parenting years. Not simply because children use screens, but because reducing them feels almost impossible even when we desperately want to.

We did not complete No iPad November perfectly.

I’m trying not to let perfection turn a partial success into a failure.

Maybe the goal is not to become the kind of family that never needs an iPad but to remain the kind of family that notices when the iPad is taking up too much room.

So, No iPad November has become Less iPad November.

Not as catchy but definitely more honest.
