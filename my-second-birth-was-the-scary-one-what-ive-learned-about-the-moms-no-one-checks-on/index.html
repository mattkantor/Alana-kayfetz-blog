My Second Birth Was the Scary One: What I've Learned About the Moms No One Checks On

By Amber Sperling, MSW, RSW, PMH-C, Founder of MARSHA Care

I always knew I wanted to be a mom, yet like so many others, I had no idea what that actually meant. Our first daughter was born by emergency C-section. We joke that she didn't want to leave the comforts of my body and had to be evicted at 41 weeks and 5 days. After the induction, her heart rate dropped with each contraction, and everything moved fast to get her out. And still, looking back, that was not the birth that really stuck with me.

Our second daughter was born a couple of years later. Very different births, very different personalities. She arrived by VBAC, almost to the minute of our scheduled C-section. To the shock of my midwife team, she came full force, with contractions starting around 2:30 a.m. and a very intense water breaking at the hospital later that morning. THAT was the birth that shook me.

In thanks to my team, specifically my midwife and lead nurse in my first birth experience, I felt in control and informed with the C-Section.  My second birth was fast, intense and the changes happening within my body I didn’t quite understand and continued to shock me.  Not to mention we had no time for an epidural and the gas was not turned on when the mask was handed to me to help reduce my contraction pain with my second.

From the outside, my VBAC was the success story, the birth everyone had hoped for. Nobody would have guessed it was the one I'd carry with me.

This lesson has stayed with me as a therapist specializing in perinatal mental health. A birth experience isn't defined by what others see or interpret. Your experience is your own, and only you can decide what felt "off," "terrifying," "out of control," or any of the many other words I hear in my practice. So many moms come in telling me they should "just move on." It is an honour to hold space for what they experienced and to affirm that however they feel about their birth is the right way for them to feel.

When the birth "went fine"

Birth trauma is more common than most of us realize. Research out of the UK suggests about 1 in 20 women develop PTSD after giving birth, and it can happen even when the birth was medically uncomplicated. Trauma isn't only about what happened. It's about whether you felt safe, heard and supported while it was happening.

Birth is also one of the only hard experiences where people respond with "Congratulations!" That makes it really hard to say, "Actually, I'm not okay." So a lot of moms don't. Or they are afraid if others know they aren’t ok CAS might be called or they will be judged.  Sometimes it gets called postpartum depression or anxiety, and the birth itself never gets talked about.

A few signs I want every mom to know so they can reach out for help:

  • Replaying the birth, or getting pulled right back into it by a smell, a sound or a place

  • Avoiding the hospital, your birth photos, or other people's birth stories

  • Feeling numb, on edge, or just not like yourself

  • Dreading another pregnancy, or deciding you could never do it again

  • Feeling sadness, panic, anxiety or other intense emotions related to anniversaries of the birth (monthly or yearly – however it shows up for you)

Birth trauma processing is a gift I am honoured to use repeatedly. The cool thing about processing past trauma is that it isn't about changing our memory or changing the event. It's about changing the amount of distress we feel when we think about the event today. When we experience something traumatic, whether big T or little t, a part of us gets stuck in that moment and can’t move forward. Trauma therapy like EMDR helps us look back on what happened from a place of safety, and through a new lens.

It doesn't have to be "big" to count

For me, feeding was the little t that nobody talks about. Our feeding journey included tube feeding and taking meds that was meant to help but left me even more anxious than I already was.  Topping up with formula was not in my plan, taking my baby to get weighed more regularly was not in my plan.  On paper, it was "just" a feeding challenge. In my body, it was a lot.

Little t traumas stack up quietly: a hard feeding start, a scary moment in the middle of the night, a comment from a provider that stuck with you. None of them has to be "bad enough" to deserve care. If it's still sitting with you, it matters.

Pregnant again, and the second time around

If you're pregnant again after a hard birth, the fear that shows up is real. Fear of birth after a traumatic one is common, and it is treatable. I know many moms who said they would never go through birth again changing their family plans.  As my own story shows, it doesn't always follow the script. Sometimes the birth everyone expects to be the healing one is the hardest. Processing your first birth before the next one can change how you approach it and empower you to ask for what you need.

Then there's the reality of the second (or third) baby. The meal trains get shorter. The check-ins slow down. People assume you've got this, and research backs up what moms tell me: we tend to get less support with our second child than with our first, right when the load doubles.

With a toddler and a newborn at home, my midwife gave me something I didn't know I needed: permission. Permission not to do all the things the second time around.   She gave me the option of not doing all the things related to breastfeeding that were challenges the first time, to not carry guilt for the reduced attention my toddler was getting, to prioritize my own needs to better be present for my kids.  That permission was support, and I'm passing it on to you. You don't have to do it the way you did it the first time. You don't have to do it all.

Where to start

You don't have to be in crisis to deserve support. In a 2024 Statistics Canada survey, about 1 in 5 Canadian moms said no provider ever asked about their emotional well-being during pregnancy or after birth. So if no one has asked you yet, I'm asking.

  • Say it out loud. Tell your doctor, midwife or nurse practitioner how you're really feeling, including about your birth.

  • Check your benefits. Therapy with a Registered Social Worker is covered by many extended health plans.

  • Find perinatal-trained support. At MARSHA Care, we offer birth trauma therapy and EMDR, a monthly Birth Trauma Support Group you can join in person in Barrie or online, and Connected Beginnings, a virtual group for moms in their third trimester. We see clients in Barrie, Orillia, Toronto and virtually across Ontario.

The question that matters most isn't "How was the birth?" It's "How are you?" Whatever your answer is, it's the right one.

Amber Sperling, MSW, RSW, PMH-C, is the founder and Clinical Director of MARSHA Care, a trauma-informed women's mental health practice in Barrie and Orillia, Ontario, with virtual care across the province. An EMDRIA Approved Consultant-in-Training, she specializes in perinatal mental health, birth trauma and EMDR.
